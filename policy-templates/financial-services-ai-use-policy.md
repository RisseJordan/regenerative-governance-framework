## Important notice on regulatory currency

The regulatory landscape for AI in financial services is active and evolving. This template reflects guidance current as of May 2026, including:

- CFPB Circular 2022-03: Adverse Action Notification Requirements and AI
- CFPB Circular 2023-03: Adverse Action Notification Requirements for Credit Decisions Using AI
- CFPB Comment to Treasury on AI in Financial Services (August 12, 2024)
- AVM Final Rule (approved August 2024)
- Colorado SB 26-189 (passed May 9, 2026, awaiting governor's signature — expected effective January 1, 2027)

The CFPB has stated clearly: there are no exceptions to federal consumer financial protection laws for new technologies. AI tools used in credit decisions, customer service, or any consumer-facing context are subject to the same legal standards as any other method.

Check [consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/advanced-technology](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/advanced-technology/) regularly for updates.

## 1. Purpose

This policy establishes how `[ORGANIZATION]` uses artificial intelligence (AI) tools — including AI-powered underwriting and decisioning models, embedded AI features in existing platforms, generative AI tools, and automated chatbots — in ways that protect consumers, maintain regulatory compliance, and reduce the risk of discrimination and unfair treatment.

AI tools offer real operational benefits for financial services organizations. They can streamline underwriting, reduce processing time, improve fraud detection, and support customer service. They also carry significant regulatory and reputational risk — particularly in areas of fair lending, adverse action transparency, and data privacy — that require active, documented management.

This policy is designed to make AI use at `[ORGANIZATION]` legally defensible, transparent, and fair. It is not designed to prevent AI adoption. It is designed to make sure adoption happens with the governance in place to protect the organization and the consumers it serves.

---

## 2. Scope

This policy applies to:

- All staff, contractors, vendors, and business partners of `[ORGANIZATION]`
- All AI tools used in any aspect of our work — credit decisioning, underwriting, customer service, collections, marketing, fraud detection, or operations
- AI tools provided by third-party vendors as well as tools staff access independently
- Automated decision-making systems, machine learning models, and generative AI tools used in any consumer-facing or consumer-impacting context

This policy applies regardless of whether the AI tool was formally adopted by the organization or is being used informally by individual staff members.

## 3. Definitions

**Artificial intelligence (AI) tool** — Any software that uses machine learning, large language models, predictive algorithms, or similar technology to generate outputs including decisions, recommendations, risk scores, summaries, or predictions.

**Adverse action** — As defined under ECOA and Regulation B: any denial of credit, change in credit terms, or other action unfavorable to a consumer's credit application or existing credit relationship.

**Adverse action notice** — The legally required notification to a consumer explaining the specific reasons for an adverse action, as required under ECOA/Regulation B and the FCRA.

**Disparate impact** — When an AI tool or model produces outcomes that disproportionately disadvantage consumers based on a protected characteristic (race, color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age, or receipt of public assistance income) even without discriminatory intent.

**Disparate treatment** — When an AI tool or model treats consumers differently based on a protected characteristic.

**Nontraditional data** — Data inputs used in AI models that are not typically found in a consumer's credit file or credit application, such as behavioral data, device data, or social indicators.

**High-risk AI use** — Any use of an AI tool that influences a consequential decision affecting a consumer's access to credit, financial products, or services.

## 4. Core principles

`[ORGANIZATION]` is committed to using AI in ways that are:

**Lawful** — All AI tools used in consumer-facing or consumer-impacting contexts must comply with ECOA, Regulation B, the FCRA, the CFPA, and all applicable state laws. There are no exceptions for AI or algorithmic tools.

**Explainable** — When AI contributes to an adverse action against a consumer, `[ORGANIZATION]` must be able to provide specific, accurate reasons for that action. Vague or generic explanations are not legally sufficient.

**Fair** — AI tools must not produce discriminatory outcomes — whether through disparate treatment or disparate impact — based on any protected characteristic under applicable fair lending laws.

**Transparent** — Consumers have a right to understand when AI has influenced a decision that affects them. Staff must understand what AI tools are in use and what their limitations are.

**Accountable** — Every AI tool used in a consumer-impacting context has a designated owner at `[ORGANIZATION]` responsible for its appropriate use, ongoing monitoring, and compliance review.

## 5. Permitted uses of AI

The following uses of AI are permitted at `[ORGANIZATION]` subject to the safeguards in this policy:

- Credit risk assessment and underwriting support through approved, documented models with human review
- Fraud detection and monitoring where outputs are reviewed by qualified staff before action is taken
- Customer service automation for routine, non-consequential inquiries
- Document processing and data extraction where human review follows
- Internal operational tasks that do not directly affect consumer decisions
- Marketing and outreach tools that have been reviewed for fair lending compliance

All permitted uses must be documented in the AI use inventory maintained by `[ROLE]`.

---

## 6. Prohibited uses of AI

The following uses of AI are prohibited at `[ORGANIZATION]`:

- Using AI to make final credit decisions without qualified human review capability and documented override process
- Using AI models that incorporate nontraditional data inputs without documented business justification and fair lending testing
- Using AI-generated adverse action reasons that are vague, generic, or do not reflect the actual basis for the decision
- Using AI tools in consumer-facing contexts that misrepresent the consumer as interacting with a human
- Using AI tools in credit decisioning that have not been reviewed for disparate impact against protected classes
- Using personal accounts on consumer AI platforms for any work involving consumer financial data
- Using AI tools provided by vendors who have not agreed to appropriate data protection and compliance terms

## 7. Adverse action notice requirements

This is the highest-priority compliance obligation for AI use in lending and credit.

### 7.1 The requirement
Under ECOA (Regulation B) and the FCRA, `[ORGANIZATION]` must provide consumers with specific, accurate reasons for any adverse action. This requirement applies fully regardless of whether the decision was made by a human, an algorithm, or an AI model.

### 7.2 What this means for AI tools
The CFPB has made clear that creditors cannot use vague or generic reasons for adverse actions when AI was involved in the decision. If an AI model identified a specific factor — for example, a payment pattern, a debt-to-income ratio derived from nontraditional data, or a behavioral indicator — that factor must be specifically identified in the adverse action notice.

`[ORGANIZATION]` must be able to:
- Identify the specific factors the AI model weighted most heavily in producing an adverse outcome
- Translate those factors into plain-language reasons a consumer can understand and act on
- Verify that those reasons accurately reflect the actual basis for the decision — not a generic checklist

Using standard CFPB sample adverse action forms is not sufficient if those forms do not reflect the actual reasons produced by the AI model.

### 7.3 Documentation requirement
Every adverse action involving an AI model must be documented with:
- The model or tool used
- The key factors identified by the model
- The specific reasons provided to the consumer
- The name of the staff member who reviewed the output before the notice was issued

## 8. Fair lending compliance

### 8.1 Testing requirements
Any AI model used in credit decisioning at `[ORGANIZATION]` must be tested for disparate impact and disparate treatment before deployment and on a regular basis thereafter. Testing must include:

- Analysis of outcomes by race, color, national origin, sex, marital status, age, and receipt of public assistance income
- Review of nontraditional data inputs for proxy risk — inputs that correlate with protected characteristics even if not explicitly race or sex
- Documentation of business justification for each model factor

### 8.2 Vendor model responsibility
If `[ORGANIZATION]` uses a vendor-provided AI model for credit decisioning, the organization remains responsible for that model's fair lending compliance. Vendor responsibility does not substitute for `[ORGANIZATION]`'s own compliance obligations.

`[ORGANIZATION]` will:
- Require vendors to provide documentation of fair lending testing for any AI model used in credit decisions
- Conduct independent review of vendor models at least annually
- Document the results of that review and any corrective actions taken

### 8.3 Model governance
Each AI model used in credit decisioning must have:
- A designated model owner at `[ORGANIZATION]`
- Documented purpose, inputs, and outputs
- A defined review and validation cycle
- A process for staff to flag concerns about model outputs

## 9. Customer service AI requirements

AI tools used in customer service — including chatbots, automated voice systems, and AI-assisted customer communications — must meet the following requirements:

- Consumers must not be misled into believing they are speaking with a human when they are interacting with an AI system
- AI customer service tools must be able to transfer consumers to a human representative upon request
- AI customer service tools must not provide inaccurate information about consumers' rights, account terms, or available options
- Consumer complaints or disputes raised through AI channels must be routed to human review within `[TIMEFRAME]`

The CFPB has identified AI chatbots that provide inaccurate information or prevent consumers from reaching human representatives as a potential UDAAP (unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts or practices) violation.

## 10. Data governance and consumer privacy

### 10.1 Nontraditional data
AI models that use nontraditional data — behavioral data, device signals, location data, social indicators, or other data not typically found in a credit file — must be reviewed for:
- Proxy risk: does this data correlate with a protected characteristic?
- Business justification: is there a documented, legitimate business reason for using this data?
- Consumer awareness: are consumers informed that this data may be used?

### 10.2 Data minimization
AI tools used in consumer-facing or consumer-impacting contexts should access only the consumer data necessary for their stated purpose. `[ORGANIZATION]` will maintain documentation of what data each AI tool accesses and why.

### 10.3 Third-party data sources
Any third-party data source used as an input to an AI model must be identified, documented, and reviewed for accuracy and fair lending implications before use.

---

## 11. Staff responsibilities

All staff who use AI tools in their work are responsible for:

- Reading and understanding this policy before using any AI tool in a consumer-impacting context
- Using only AI tools that appear on the approved tool list for consumer-facing or credit-related work
- Reviewing all AI-generated adverse action reasons before they are communicated to consumers
- Reporting any concern about an AI tool's output — including outputs that seem discriminatory, inaccurate, or inconsistent with known facts — to `[ROLE]`
- Never representing to a consumer that an AI system is a human representative
- Completing AI governance and fair lending training as required by `[ORGANIZATION]`

---

## 12. Incident response

If an AI tool causes or contributes to a potential fair lending violation, consumer harm, regulatory complaint, or data incident, the following steps apply:

1. The staff member who identifies the issue reports it immediately to `[ROLE/COMPLIANCE OFFICER]`
2. Use of the AI tool in the affected context is suspended pending review
3. `[ROLE/COMPLIANCE OFFICER]` assesses whether regulatory notification obligations are triggered
4. The incident is documented in the compliance incident log
5. Root cause is identified and corrective action is implemented before the tool is returned to use
6. If the incident involves a pattern of potentially discriminatory outcomes, it is escalated to `[ROLE]` and legal counsel is notified

## 13. Colorado regulatory note

Colorado SB 26-189 (passed May 9, 2026, awaiting governor's signature) replaces the original SB 24-205 and is expected to take effect January 1, 2027. For financial services organizations operating in Colorado, the new law focuses on "covered automated decision-making technology" (ADMT) used in consequential decisions — including lending and financial services — and requires consumer disclosures, explanation of adverse outcomes, correction rights, and access to human review.

`[ORGANIZATION]` will monitor the governor's signature and any Attorney General rulemaking and update this policy and related practices accordingly before the January 1, 2027 effective date.
